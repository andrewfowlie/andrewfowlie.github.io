Welcome to my slidedeck on Quantum Mechanics for a non-mathematical audience --- _Quantum Mechanics In Your Face For Anybody!_ There are no equations and little maths beyond percentages. The key conceptual differences between Quantum and Classical Physics are here, though, and these topics require attention and serious thought.

The title alludes to two wonderful expositions of quantum mechanics by the masters. Mermin and Coleman are both physicists' physicists: they aren't widely known to the public, but they contributed tremendous insight and clarity. Feynman described Mermin's paper as "one of the most beautiful papers in physics." You are encouraged to read [Bringing Home the Atomic World: Quantum Mysteries for Anybody](https://doi.org/10.1119/1.12594)  
and watch [Sidney Coleman’s Dirac Lecture: “Quantum Mechanics in Your Face”](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EtyNMlXN-sw).

## 🤖 Chatbots In Your Face For Anybody

<div class="pdf-card">
  <div class="pdf-label">SLIDEDECK</div>
  <p class="pdf-links">
    Coming soon.
  </p>
</div>

I take a fun look at the history and workings of chatbots, starting from the 1960s rule-based models such as ELIZA up to present transformer-based large language models such as ChatGPT.
I will explain the principles and working parts of these models as simply as I can, and with as little maths as I can, whilst hopefully retaining substantive and interesting explanations.

No prior knowledge of machine learning or advanced maths is required; some linear algebra would help, but come even if you don't know any. By the end of the talk, we should have an understanding of the whole pipeline of a GPT from input to output, including how input text is represented in maths, why
attention matters, what a transformer is, and why chatbots hallucinate.

## ↕️ Which Way Is Up?

I introduce vector calculus for undergraduates by working through the question _which way is up?_ Some knowledge of differentiation is assumed, but not much. We end by showing how our findings are applied in optimization algorithms, such as gradient descent, and machine learning.

I've written this "little red book" from memory and as a tribute to a similar one given to me as an undergraduate at the University of Durham in 2005 by, I believe, Dr. Tom. We were constantly referring to them during our lab work and report writing. Of course, the errors present in this work are my own. I hope this version will stand students in good stead, as Dr.  Tom's one did for me. It is intended to be used by today's undergraduate students during laboratory sessions.
